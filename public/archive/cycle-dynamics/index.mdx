If you are someone who is assigned male at birth, odds are pretty strong that you might not be too intimately familiar with the female menstrual cycle. Growing up presumed male and as someone who didn't have any sisters, my education of the subject was pretty limited. My parents are both evangelical christians, so discussions about anything surrounding female anatomy were extremely limited until I took sex education in primary school. Even that education tended to be pretty sparse about details, focusing only on the anatomy and the rudamentary organic processes. Culturally I knew what PMS is and that once a month women would get cranky, but most of that came through the lens of patriarchy and systemic misogyny as something that inconvenienced men. The topic of female libido was even less discussed, outside of crude jokes in sitcoms and sexist cliches in films.

So, if you're a transfeminine person who is six to twelve months into hormone therapy and have noticed some weird symptoms that you can't trace to anything you've actively done and seem to happen on a roughly 28 day cycle, you might be a little bit weirded out. Perhaps you aren't trans at all, but you are close to someone who is on estrogen HRT and have noticed an odd pattern of behavior. Or maybe you're just someone who has heard that trans women can get periods and you want to know more.

This guide is for you.

First lets get this out of the way... Yes, transfeminine people (meaning people assigned male at birth who medically transition to a female phenotype) can develop a monthly period cycle.

No, we do not menstruate, that would be physically impossible. I am not going to spend time justifying this, it's been done better by others. At this point, anyone who rejects this is just denying the lived experiences of millions of people, and they can go suck an egg. The purpose of this post is to explain the biological reasons for why it happens.

Also, what I will describe below does not just affect trans people, it is true for _anyone_ whose body runs on estrogen, including people assigned female at birth who no longer have a uterus and/or ovaries following a hysterectomy.

### What's in a period?

Obviously, the first thing people think of when talking about periods is menses, the outflow of blood and uterine tissue through the vagina. This, however, is but one of the many cyclical symptoms that people who experience periods go through. There are over a dozen other common cyclical symptoms that are associated with periods:

- Cramping in the lower abdomen
- Water retention and Bloating.
- Gas, loose stools and other intestinal issues ("period shits")
- Nausea
- Irritability ([PMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premenstrual_syndrome)), mood swings, irrational thoughts, depression, or even suicidal ideation ([PMDD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premenstrual_dysphoric_disorder))
- Muscle aches and pains, join pain, stiffness
- Changes to sexual arousal
- Changes in ADHD symptoms, brain fog
- Breast engorgement, soreness, and nipple tenderness
- Acne outbreaks
- Fatigue
- Joint Pain
- Endometrial abdominal pain

Not every one experiences the entire list of symptoms, every body is different, but all of these are common among cisgender women and transfeminine individuals alike, regardless of the presence of a uterus or ovaries. Some can happen in connection with medication cycles, it is really common for transfems who are just starting HRT to notice mood swings at the peaks and troughs of their medication dose, for example. My cisgender wife is on estrogen patches and will have an emotional drop on the day she is due to change her patch. That isn't the pattern we're talking about in this essay.

A typical period cycle lasts roughly four weeks. This varies from person to person, and can even vary month to month in reaction to medication changes, changes in diet, stressors, and other factors. Traditionally the cycle length is measured as the time between the start of menses (blood flow), but in someone who doesn't have a uterus it is necessary to watch for other symptoms.

Myself, I mark the start of my cycle based on my mental state; I will have a day or two of elevated [DPDR](/en/biochemical-dysphoria/) symptoms that follows several days of cramping and bloating. Sometimes I will, ironically, have a day of really high energy and body confidence right before the emotional fall. I make use of a period tracker app on my phone to record my symptoms and then mark the cycle start once I am confident that I've hit that cycle start. Some trackers support marking the cycle explicitly (Clue just added a mode for people who do not menstruate, for example) but in those that don't you can just mark blood flow.

The first two weeks of the cycle are termed the *Follicular Phase*, so named because in someone with ovaries, this is when the cycle encourages the ovarian follicules to reach towards maturation. The thing that I was never told, growing up, was what the end of the follicular phase is like, emotionally. Around day ten of the cycle the body enters what many tracking apps refer to as the "fertile window", a 5-7 day length of time where unprotected sex is most likely to result in a conception. During this time the hypothalmus [floods the brain with Oxytocin and Vasopressin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_hormones_on_sexual_motivation#Oxytocin_and_vasopressin), prompting a motivation towards coupling behavior.

This not only causes a sharp rise in libido, but it also has a powerful influence on sexual attraction. This can be quite distressing to someone who is exclusively attracted to women, since the brain starts urging towards male attention and... copulation. Some of my friends have taken to calling this "Bicanthropy", because it feels like the lunar cycle makes them multi-sexual. I have heard many-a-lesbian complain about suddenly feeling urges to flirt with male partners, only for that interest to completely dry up the next day.

Additionally, it can cause a powerful urge for attention from whatever partner you have, and if that partner is not available or isn't interested, this does not blend well with [rejection sensitivities](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/up-and-running/202603/what-you-should-know-about-rejection-sensitive-dysphoria). My wife and I came to refer to this time of the month as *Danger Week*, because my neediness would become self destructive. People who grow up female assigned will experience this during their teen years, when the social risks are lower and they have less responsibility to juggle. It's quite a bit more awkward to go through when you're an adult. Thankfully this eases off once you're on the other side of second puberty. 

All of this reaches a crescendo at the cycle midpoint, roughly day 14 or 15, when the body attempts to trigger ovulation. From then on we are in the Luteal Phase and mental state returns to baseline for about a week. In a conventional female reproductive system, this is a time where estrogen and progesterone levels are rising, creating more mental clarity and confidence, peaking around day 21, before begining a descent in the final week leading up to the end of the cycle. Personally, I have noticed that my breasts often feel larger and heavier about seven days before the cycle ends. I will have a day or two of PMS somewhere in that final week, where I catch myself irrationally iritated with my family members, annoyed by tiny inconveniences that usually wouldn't even register, or elevated road rage while out driving.

So where is all this coming from? Well there are several sources of these symptoms, so lets first dive deep into what causes the cycle in the first place.

### An introduction to the gonadotropin feedback system

It all starts deep inside the brain in a region known as [rostral periventricular region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periventricular_nucleus) of the [third ventricle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_ventricle). Yes that's a long name, no you don't need to know it. It's a thin layer of cells in the center of the brain that is responsible for managing the reproductive systems of the human body. It contains receptors for numerous hormones that are distributed to the body via the blood stream, but for this topic we're mostly interested in Androgens, Estrogens, and Progestogins.

Based on the levels of those three hormones, the periventricular nucleus expresses proteins called Kisspeptins, specifically Kisspeptin-GPR54, a [peptide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peptide) which instructs cells within the [hypothalamus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothalamus) to secrete [Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonadotropin-releasing_hormone). GnRH1 is then received by the pituitary gland, which in turn releases two other hormones into the blood stream: [Luteinizing Hormone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luteinizing_hormone) (LH) and [Follicle Stimulating Hormone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Follicle-stimulating_hormone) (FSH). These two are the main signaling mechanism that the brain uses to tell other organs in the body what to do to maintain sexual function.

In people with testes, FSH supports the maturation of sperm. In people with ovaries, FSH stimulates the maturation of [ovarian follicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovarian_follicle). As the follicles reach maturation, they start to excrete large amounts of estradiol, causing the first estradiol surge during the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle.

LH causes the production of testosterone in both the testes and the ovaries.  However, in the ovaries that testosterone is immediately metabolized into estrogens. When ovarian follicles reach maturation for the month they become more sensitive to LH, allowing for an LH surge to induce luteinization (hence the name). Luteinization causes one or more mature follicles to pop, releasing the ovum (egg) and forming into a [corpus luteum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_luteum), which produces progesterone and even more estrogens. Additionally, the adrenal glands also produce testosterone in reaction to LH levels [[cite]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0303720710002832), [[cite]](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/7716).

The progesterone, testosterone, and estradiol enter the blood stream and find their way back to the periventricular nucleus where their concentration is measured as negative feedback. The more of the collective three hormones are present, the less Kisspeptin is produced, meaning less GnRH, and less LH and FSH.

The hypothalamus pulses GnRH1 approximately every 60 to 120 minutes. The frequency of the pulses effects which hormone is released ([lower frequency skews towards FSH and high frequency triggers LH](https://web.archive.org/web/20150923190449/http://www.biolreprod.org/content/56/4/1012.full.pdf)). The intensity of the pulse controls how much of each hormone is produced.

In people with male levels of estrogen the GnRH pulses are fairly constant, producing a steady rise and fall in gonad function across the day [[cite](https://journals.bioscientifica.com/joe/article-abstract/52/1/51/9652/STUDIES-ON-THE-RELATIONSHIP-BETWEEN-PLASMA)]. At a critical threshold of estradiol levels, [the GNRH1-5 genes activate a genomic response](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2744362/), [inhibiting Kisspeptin production](https://www.ibroneuroreports.org/article/S2667-2421(23)01604-4/fulltext) and causing fluctuations in GnRH rhythms, essentially changing what the brain perceives as its proper baseline on a day to day basis over the course of a monthly cycle. These rhythms cause the rise and fall of LH and FSH in the patterns which stimulate the ovulation and menstrual cycle, including the dramatic spike that occurs half way into the cycle in order to induce ovulation.

**In short, the hypothalamus has two modes, androgen dominant and estrogen dominant. If you're running on estrogen, your genes tells the hypothalamus to cycle your hormone levels, regardless of if you actually have ovaries.** The hypothalamus doesn't know if the organs are there to listen to those signals, it just knows how much estrogen it expects to be in the blood stream, and it responds accordingly with regulation of GnRH output.

But here lies the rub: Transfem estradiol and progesterone levels should not rise and fall with these cycles, right? Injection and transdermal dosages have rise and falls on the scale of 1-2 weeks, and those on pills experience it on the scale of hours, nothing approaching the roughly 28 day long menstrual cycle. And yet, many transfems I've spoken to (as well as myself) have experienced problems with blood level inconsistency. With no change in dosage, someone will get a blood test done one month and then another 90 days later and get completely different numbers, even when being careful to get their blood drawn at the same point in their medication cycle.

What could account for so much variance?

### Where we're going, we don't need ovaries.

While the primary receivers of LH and FSH are the ovaries and testes, there are [numerous tissues and organs](https://journals.bioscientifica.com/ec/article/15/8/e260232/77914/The-extragonadal-biology-of-gonadotropin-hormones) within the body which also contain receptors for the gonadotropin hormones. Bones, skin, fat, neurons in the brain, nervous tissue, immune cells, [endometrial cells](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29974367/), the prostate, [adrenal glands](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3459175/), [mammary glands](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0024320597000738), and [other breast tissues](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/89/8/4119/2844748?login=false). Remember those last two, that's important for later.

There are four forms of estrogen: estrone (E1), estradiol (E2), estriol (E3), and estetrol (E4). E3 and E4 are only relevant for pregnancy, so we can ignore those. The primary estrogen that the majority of the body reacts to is estradiol. When we talk about the behavior of estrogen receptors, almost all the time we're talking about estradiol, because estrone barely even fits into the receptor-- it only has a binding affinity of 0.6% of what estradiol has. This effectively means that estrone in the blood stream is inert, it just sits there until it is metabolized into one of the other estrogens.

This process of converting estradiol and estrone back and forth is performed by enzymes called [17β-Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/17%CE%B2-Hydroxysteroid_dehydrogenase) (HSD17B). There are fifteen different kinds of HSD17B, but the first two (HSD17B1 and HSD17B2) are the ones we're interested in. The first catalyzes estradiol into estrone, and the second does the opposite. Both forms of HSD17B are expressed by cells found in breast tissue. The same tissue found to contain LH receptors.

By expressing these two enzymes, breast tissue is able to shunt active estradiol in the blood stream over into inert estrone, and then back again as a means of regulating the amount of active estradiol present to match the levels called for by the hypothalamus. That's the first part of this mystery, and it's what accounts for the rapid drop in estradiol levels at the end of both the follicular and luteal phases of the cycle. This process works regardless of if the estradiol came from the ovaries or from medication. The exception to this is ethinyl-estradiol, which is the synthetic estrogen used in birth control. EE is able to slot into estrogen receptors just the same as bioidentical estradiol, but the extra ethynyl group on the molecule prevents metabolization by HSD17B.

Because this HSD17B expression requires available breast tissue, this explains why the transfems who do experience these symptoms don't start to feel them until breast development has yielded results. Indeed, in my own conversations with well over a hundred transfems, the ones who do not experience period cycles tend to be those who have also had very poor breast development.

### What does all this have to do with period symptoms?

All of the mental symptoms of periods can be attributed to a drop in available estradiol. Many transfems just starting on HRT experience PMS quite frequently just from the rise and fall of their medications while their bodies build up base levels. Acne and other skin changes can also be attributed to sudden drops in available estradiol.

In women with ovaries, the endometrium layer of the uterus that forms during the first half of the luteal phase of the cycle is rich with a lipid named [Prostaglandin F2alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostaglandin_F2alpha) (PGF2a). When the uterine lining breaks down, all that PGF2a is released into the surrounding tissue, as well as into the corpus luteum and the ovum, catalyzing the destruction of the cell. The released PGF2a causes contractions in [the muscle layer that surrounds the uterus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myometrium).

Ok, so what does that have to do with people who don't have uteruses? F2a is not the only prostaglandin that causes muscle contractions. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) also causes muscle spasms in sufficient quantities, and is used to induce labor because of this effect. PGE2 is produced basically anywhere in the body that is under attack or has been damaged, but it is also formed in muscles, the intestines, adipose fat, and the [mammary glands](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/16204198). Additionally, both PGE2 and PGF2a are produced by [the thyroid and the adrenal glands](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/j.1476-5381.1967.tb02003.x). As shown above, mammary glands and the adrenal glands have both been shown to respond to fluctuations in LH concentrations.

Adrenal released prostaglandins cause contractions in the smooth muscle lining the intestines and abdomen, inflammation in the abdomen from the protaglandins would induce bloating, and prostaglandin surges in mammary tissue would explain breast engorgement and sensitivity. Both Prostaglandins and reduced estradiol account for joint and muscle pain.

Some may scoff at the idea that these secretions could be sufficient to produce these symptoms, but the fact is we don't know. Nobody has studied prostaglandin secretion in women who do not have ovaries. I cannot find a single paper even acknowledging the phenomenon.

### Here there be dragons

There's a lot about all of this that we don't know. At the time of writing this, modern trans medical care is approaching two decades old (while trans hormone therapy has been around for well over a century, WPATH in its current incarnation has only existed since 2007, and the current Standards of Care, which were a significant improvement over previous versions, came out in 2011). Prior to that, transgender care was largely ignored or considered a fringe practice. Additionally, the trans population is _exploding_ in size right now due to the rise in awareness, people are coming out and starting hormone therapy in record numbers. Estimates of total transgender individuals [more than doubled](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/07/01/health/transgender-population.html?_r=0) in the 2010s, and [have doubled again](https://communityinsights.hrc.org/research/lgbtq-population-estimates) in the 2020s. Suddenly we're learning all sorts of new things about the medical behavior of transgender individuals, but also medical science is just starting to pay attention. The medical community [hadn't even officially recognized that transfems could breastfeed](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/02/15/health/transgender-woman-breast-feed.html) until 2018, which the trans community had known for decades.

Unfortunately, as far as I can find, no one is studying transfem periods. Many existing notions for how womens bodies work are based on the assumption that estrogen and progesterone fluctuations cause these reactions. The only research I could find on extra-gonadal FSH interactions involved its role in bone density. I couldn't even find data on LH and FSH levels in transfems, just a general expectation that it will be significantly lower than cis women because of the elevated estrogen levels. There is no proven correlation between LH and HSD17B expression, only that the receptors exist in the same cells that express it.

And it seems that this may be the case for some time. Due to the rise in right-wing policy across western society, we're seeing a significant suppression of research funding for everything and anything connected to minority medical study. This is especially true for trans people, but it is also affecting research that centers intersex conditions, people of color, and women's anatomy. It may be another decade or more before we finally get any further answers on this subject.
