# 트랜지션할 가치가 있을까요?

<Note>
  **큐레이터의 메세지**: 다음 내용은 훌륭한 작가인 닥 임파서블<sup>(Doc Impossible)</sup>이 쓴 [후회, 몇 가지가 있긴 했지만](https://stainedglasswoman.substack.com/p/regrets-ive-had-a-few)의 상당 부분을 발췌한 것으로, 저자의 허락을 받아 재공유한 것입니다. [스테인드글래스 여성<sup>(Stained Glass Woman)</sup>](https://stainedglasswoman.substack.com/)에서 그녀의 글을 더 읽어볼 수 있습니다.
</Note>

사람들이 트랜지션 과정에서 느끼는 후회에 대한 두려움을 이야기할 때, 보통 그 속에는 잘못된 이분법적 사고가 깔려있는 경우가 많습니다. 완벽하고 더없이 행복해지거나, 혹은 극도로 불행해져서 디트랜지션하고 후회로 가득한 삶은 산다는 사고입니다. 여기에서 그 사이의 중간 지점은 존재하지 않습니다. 그래서 우리 중 많은 사람들이 스스로의 존재 자체를 의심하는 회의적인 세상에 스스로가 존재할 자격이 있다는 것을 증명하기 위해 매 순간 반항적으로 당당하게 행복을 외쳐야 한다고 느끼곤 합니다.

음. 저는 개인적으로 이게 좀 잔인하다고 생각합니다.

그래서 저는 여러분과 이 트랜지션 과정에서 어떤 것들을 느끼게 되는지 진지한 대화를 나누고 싶습니다. [믿음의 도약<sup>(leap of faith)</sup>](https://stainedglasswoman.substack.com/p/leap-of-faith)을 할 용기를 찾았을 때, 거의 틀림없이 여러분 자신의 트랜지션 과정에서도 마주치게 될 것이니까요.

## 수치로 살펴봅시다

스타인드글래스 여성 글을 전에 읽어본 적이 있다면, 제가 데이터를 얼마나 중요하게 생각하시는지 알 겁니다. 거기서부터 살펴보도록 하죠. 트랜지션에 대해, 그리고 어떤 게 우리의 삶과 행복에 영향을 미치는 지 데이터는 무엇을 말하고 있을까요?

우선, 트랜스젠더 당사자를 대상으로 실시된 역대 최대 규모의 조사인 [2022 미국 트랜스젠더 설문조사](https://ustranssurvey.org/)를 살펴보겠습니다. 이 설문조사에서는 92,000명의 트랜스젠더 당사자가 참여하였고, 결과는 상당히 압도적입니다. 트랜지션한 모두의 97%가 자신의 삶이 트랜지션 이전과 비교하였을 때 동일하게 좋거나 더 나아졌다고 응답했습니다. *모든 측면*을 고려하였을 때 말입니다. 예를 들어 어떤 사람이 교통사고를 당해 앞으로 만성 통증을 겪게 되었다고 해봅시다. 그 사고가 트랜지션과 전혀 관계가 없더라도, 삶이 악화되었다고 응답한 3%에 포함되게 되는 것입니다.

Now, you might not be in medicine, so you might not, like, get what that means. Let me break it down for you: [the average regret rate of *all* surgeries is 14%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28243695/). Meanwhile, most specific research on gender-affirming surgery finds a regret rate somewhere between 0.3% and 0.5%, which means that transition surgeries have a regret rate as much as *forty-eight times lower* than the average of all surgeries.

And that? That's pretty incredible. It's the lowest regret rate for any category of surgery, and not by a small margin.

But let's zoom out: overall, not leaning on one study but instead looking at basically all of them, what does the research say about how transition turns out, both in terms of physical and emotional health? Well, the answer is, [overwhelmingly positive](https://whatweknow.inequality.cornell.edu/topics/lgbt-equality/what-does-the-scholarly-research-say-about-the-well-being-of-transgender-people/).

So… where's the catch, right? I said there were asterisks.

Yeah. There are. And those asterisks are other people.

Our unemployment rate is four times the national average in the United States. Our poverty rate is three times the national average in the United States. Employment discrimination is common, even thought we have pretty clear legal protections. But, you knew that already. Probably not the numbers, sure, but the heart of it. It's why you're worried you'll regret it at all, why you're fighting the call of transition.

It's why you're reading this article, in the end, isn't it?

So, here's the part I want you to think about: those folks from that table there? Where one in ten were fired just for being trans? Compare that with the earlier table, where only 3% of people said their life was worse in any way since they transitioned. You know how awful it is to get fired, and to get fired for who you are is way, way worse. And yet, almost everyone in this study *who* was fired for being trans *still said transition was a net positive*.

Think for a minute just how good the good parts must be to outweigh just that.

Yeah.

Like I said. It's a mixed bag, and also it's the most wonderful thing you're ever going to do. Both things can be true at the same time.

## But what if I get it wrong and detransition?

That's the other part I hear— "how can I be sure I got it right?" "What if I detransition? Won't I regret everything?"

Okay. Let's talk about detransition. And I promise, the reality is better than you think it'll be. Let's start off with the big numbers.

>  Of respondents who had transitioned, **9% had gone back to living as their sex assigned at birth** at some point in their lives, predominantly due to social and structural stigma
> -- *[USTS Health & Wellbeing Report](https://transequality.org/sites/default/files/2025-06/USTS_2022Health%26WellbeingReport_WEB.pdf) pg 51*

That seems bad, right? Almost one person in 10 detransitioning seems like *bad* odds. Hang in there with me, because they dug deeper and asked *why*— and those answers are really revealing.

That's a big list, but I want you to start at the very, very bottom — ***only 0.36% of everyone who started transition realized transition wasn't right for them.*** Put a different way, only 4% of everyone who detransitioned did it because transition wasn't right for them.

233 people.

Total.

Out of almost 65,000 who answered that particular question.

I want to ask you to sit with those odds for a minute, because they're staggering. Virtually everyone who detransitions only does it temporarily. They're not wrong about being trans— they just need to get to a better life situation before they can retransition. Heck, one ten-year study of a transition clinic in the UK found, of 3,000 participants, *only three* were living as their gender assigned at birth after 10 years.

So, if you're worried that you've got it wrong? Statistically speaking, you haven't. Folks who really, truly get it wrong are as so rare that, statistically speaking, they almost don't exist.

## Okay, sure, but what if it IS me? What if I end up as part of the 0.36%?

…you know what? Fair. You're calling me out on making a [gambler's fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambler%27s_fallacy) there; no matter how unlikely it is that you, specifically, aren't right about being trans, *it's your life*, and you deserve to have the impact, if you're one of the very few who do get it wrong, treated with respect.

First, let's face a big problem: I'm a scientist. I'm trying to give you the best data we have. And the fact of the matter is this: you need to round up a pretty good headcount to run a study of human behavior and produce results that can be relied upon. The problem is that every study is based *somewhere*, and recruiting people for that study, especially one where you have to do, like, interviews so you can understand how people feel in a systematic way, ends up getting limited by available headcount.

Let me illustrate real quick.

Say you based your study on detransition in New York City, one of the largest cities in the world, with a population of 8.5 million people who could potentially be willing to hop a train or drive in to your office for that interview. Now, if about 2.33% of the population is trans, as it is according to [recent research](https://www.hrc.org/press-releases/record-breaking-lgbtq-adults-now-12-of-population-greater-than-10-in-40-of-50-states), that means that you've got around 198,000 trans people living in New York City. With a got-it-wrong rate of only 0.36%, though, that means there are only 713 people *in the entirety of the New York City metropolitan area*, who are actual candidates for your study, because they transitioned, found it wasn't for them, and walked it back.

And that? That means that you've got to recruit somewhere between a third and a half of all the eligible people in the whole metropolitan area of one of the largest cities in the world to have a study with a decent enough size to give meaningful, reliable data that other people could point to and say that it's real, because studies with [small sample sizes create garbage data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11379640/).

And speaking as a researcher? If you have to recruit half of an entire regional population to make your study happen? *It ain't happening.* Just, period. Recruitment rates are just too low.

All of this is a longwinded way of explaining something pretty simple: we don't have good science that looks at about how people who detransition feel when they got it wrong. There just… aren't enough detransitioners.

So, what we have are stories.

You've probably heard of [Chloe Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloe_Cole), who has made [hundreds of thousands of dollars as a paid political anti-trans activist](https://www.erininthemorning.com/p/chloe-cole-testifies-she-makes-up) and detransitioner. She has every financial reason to lie about, well, *everything*. Be honest with yourself— how much nonsense would a $200,000 payday earn from you? That's life-changing money.

Even if we zoom out a bit, take everything that Cole says at face value: she's one story. One out of the nearly 8 million trans folks who live in America. If you're looking for one disaster story about almost *anything*, you'll find it somewhere.

People are human. We make mistakes. Your worry about that is why you're even reading this, isn't it?

Well, before Chloe Cole was a big deal, [Ky Schevers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky_Schevers) was the darling of the anti-trans movement, and danced the exact same dance Cole is right now. And you know what? Now she's transitioning again, [and is fighting against the exact folks who used to pay her bills](https://abcnews.com/US/former-detransitioner-fights-anti-transgender-movement-backed/story?id=92597182).

Turns out she wasn't wrong after all.

But I don't want to focus on the big names. Yeah, they're the stories you've probably heard, but they're not typical. You don't get into the news by being typical.

Probably the most beautiful story of detransition I've ever read comes from [Dolphin Diaries](https://bsky.app/profile/dolphindiaries.bsky.social), who transitioned and lived as a man for eight years before she detransitioned. It's a two-parter, which you can read [here](https://dolphindiaries.substack.com/p/how-to-prevent-detransition-in-five) and [here](https://dolphindiaries.substack.com/p/detransuncis-part-2)-- and *please, I'm begging you, go read it—* but I want to highlight a few parts of what she wrote there.

> …many detransitioners do not have such black-and-white feelings about their past. Some are nonbinary and unhappy with either ‘man' or ‘woman'; some do not maintain that their gender dysphoria wasn't actually real; some even reject the label ‘detrans' on principle, even though they have verifiably detransitioned.

So what does detransition look like? Well, as with everything it's a mixed bag. There are the "black-and-white" extremes of those who Got It Wrong and are mad as hell about it, like Chloe Cole… and more commonly, you have a smattering of nonbinary people and people who went back to their sex assigned at birth— "uncis," as Dolphin Diaries puts it, but who are basically just fine. It was a thing they did, and now they're not doing it anymore. That's all.

"Detransition," as she says, "is inevitable;" in any large enough group, there'll be someone who does any given thing sooner or later. That's just statistics plus time.

I want you to sit with that for a minute. It's important.

Some people will get it wrong *and that is okay*. Including if it's you. It won't be the end of your story. It won't ruin your life. It won't even be the most important part of your life, in the end. Just… a thing you did for a while, and now you're doing something else, like the overwhelming majority of detrans folks.

I mean, I'm trans. I'm a researcher and the focus of my research is on transness. Transition is the best thing I've ever done. And my transness *still* isn't the most important part of who I am; it's just one facet. An important one, sure. But just one, of many.

So, why are you so afraid of *this specific thing*, this incredibly unlikely percentage of a percentage of a percentage? Why does it haunt you so?

> Cis-ness is a regime. A status quo… To be cis is not merely to self-identify as the sex on your birth certificate; who's even looking at those? It is to live in accordance with your biological destiny, and every social law that entails. This destiny is assigned at birth, yes, but it does not end there: it follows you all the way.
>
> Cis-ness is not an identity— it is a reward for doing as you're told.

Let's unpack that a bit, because it's incredibly rich.

What Dolphin Diaries is saying here, as a detransitioner, is all about power and privilege. You know that trans folks kinda get beat up on in society, the same way Black folks get beat up on, or Muslim folks, or Jewish folks, or women, or—

I could go on. You know how things are, even if you don't like to look at them directly, because it makes you uncomfortable. People at the top tend to punch down, and transition will put you further down the pile, which means more people will get to punch down at you.

But all that means that, if you feel like she feels, if the heart of your fear, about getting it wrong, is about your place in the world, about *belonging*? All the scaremongering about "irreversible effects"? It's a shell game. There is nothing truly irreversible, biologically. For any surgery or hormone that takes you one way, there's an equal one that'll take you the other if you need. Get a vaginoplasty, to cite maybe the most extreme possible example, and decide it was the wrong move? Guess what, phalloplasties were originally invented for cis men who lost their genitals to some disaster, and vice versa.

You can get it fixed. You can go back to, in every meaningful way, the way you are now.

*So, if the effects of transition aren't what any of your terror is really about, since everything is reversible with enough effort, what are you scared about?*

> If transition was easy, known, free— more people would detransition, certainly. But that wouldn't mean much. Because they'd be people like anyone else. Their bodies — transsexual bodies — would be just the same, just as worthy. They would be real.

The fear is about *being real*, being legitimate, being treated equally in our profoundly unequal society. About being respected in your gender, in your life. It's a fear not of being wrong, *but of the consequences of being wrong*— that, after you try transition, you'll have to live with people treating you differently and of having to reverse course. You're scared of other folks, scared that you won't be treated as cis anymore.

And yeah, you're right. You won't.

It reminds me of [a very well-known talk that the scholar and activist Jane Elliott did many years ago](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/M7w17iZDiRY), where she asked a crowded hall of white folks to stand up if they'd be okay being treated the way society treats Black folks. And you know what? Not a single person stood.

Nobody.

To quote her talk, "you know what's happening, you don't want it for you. I want to know why you're so willing to accept it or allow it to happen for others?"

This is the thing that nobody really says, and I think you have a right to hear it: your fears are rational, even if you're telling yourself that it's about something it isn't, not really. The fear that you're wrong and will detransition, for most folks, is just a useful excuse that you can point to. Life for trans folks *is* harder than it is for cis folks. And yeah, that includes '"uncis" people, as Dolphin Diaries calls people who detransition.

But the fact of the matter is this: if you're trans, that's mostly genetics. Hiding in the closet won't change that fact, and it means that your life is already harder because you're trans, *has always been harder* because you're trans, *will continue* to be harder because you're trans.

That ship has sailed.

So, what in the world are you gonna do about it?

## ...what in the world am I gonna do about this?

I can give you easy-sounding answers.

- Transition! It's not one big thing, it's a million little choices, and if any of them turns out to be wrong, you can walk it back.

- Find people who won't treat you worse for being trans, for exploring transition! Whether you're right or wrong, they're the highest quality of people out there, folks you can truly be yourself around, no matter who that turns out to be.

They're both true. Statistically speaking, they're objectively correct. Even though transition is a gigantic project, and an expensive one to boot. Even though "finding better people" might mean letting some folks you were close to take themselves out of your life. Even though they might even be family. Letting go of people who only [loved you conditionally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_love) sucks almost as much as realizing that their love was conditional in the first place.

So, yeah, they sound easy, but they're not.

You've seen the numbers, you know how unlikely it is that you're sitting here, reading this, and that you're wrong. You know, deep down, that nobody anywhere is going to lead a regret-free life, either in transition or out of it, and that it's fundamentally unfair for you to hold yourself to the standard of needing to be regret-free. You have the right to try wild and weird things, to mess up on some of them, to carry some regrets and a few great stories for parties with you the rest of your life, *however your choices turn out*.

All of those things are objectively true. They're missing the important point, though, the last bit that I think you need to hear in all this.

Not choosing is a choice.

Hiding in the closet and hoping this all goes away has lasting physical and psychological consequences which will measurably reduce both the length and the quality of your life. And more importantly? Ask a hundred trans people what their greatest regret is in transition, and you'll get the same answer from 99 of them: "Not starting my transition sooner."

And please, if you read only one single link in this entire essay, [please read this one](https://web.archive.org/web/20241004153041/https://www.reddit.com/r/asktransgender/comments/88t6sg/some_tips_from_a_58_years_old_dying_trans_woman/). Please. *Please*. I'm not going to spoil it for you, but you're going to cry.

So, you're going to have regrets, no matter what choices you make. Again, that ship has sailed. That's just how life is. Trying to avoid them will only make things worse, *no matter what you do*. So, rather than trying to avoid regret, which is impossible, ask yourself a better question, a more fundamental one.

What sort of life do you want to lead?

Because it won't happen if you never try. And you can't light yourself on fire to keep other people warm. The only place that ends is with you burnt up and them cold anyway. So, which regrets can you live with? The regret of having tried something big to make your life better, and of having it not work out in the end?

Or the regret of having never lived your own life at all?
